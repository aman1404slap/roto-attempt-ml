# S2 — taking away what we tell it about the shapes themselves

*Read [s0.md](s0.md) first, then [s1.md](s1.md). This one follows on.*

*For the blow-by-blow of each training attempt and what it cost, see
[s2-attempts.md](s2-attempts.md).*

---

## The one-sentence version

Until now we have been handing the model a cheat sheet about every shape. S2 takes away two
lines of it.

## What was on the cheat sheet

For every shape in a layer, we currently tell the model:

1. **which shape this is** — a name, effectively
2. **how many control points it has** — the little dots an artist drags, between 4 and 68 of them
3. **which frames it is on screen for** — its lifespan

S2 takes away **2 and 3**. The model has to work both out by looking at the cutout.

Number 1 stays for now. Taking that away too is the stage after this one, and it is the big one.

## Why bother, when the output looks the same either way?

Because that cheat sheet is the difference between a demo and a tool.

Today, to run this on a new shot, someone would have to tell it how many shapes there are, how
many points each one has, and when each appears and disappears. If you already knew all that,
you would have done most of the thinking already. The whole direction of this project is
removing those lines one at a time until you can hand it a cutout and nothing else.

S2 does not make the picture better. If it goes perfectly, the picture is *exactly the same* and
the model needs two fewer things told to it. That is the win.

---

## Why lifespans are genuinely hard

Here is the thing that makes this stage difficult, and it is the same thing that makes the whole
project difficult.

**A layer's cutout is the union of all its shapes.** Twenty overlapping shapes get flattened into
one white blob. Looking at the blob, you cannot see where one shape ends and the next begins —
that information was destroyed when they were merged.

So when a shape switches off in the middle of a pile of overlapping shapes, **the picture does
not change at all.** The evidence simply is not there. No amount of cleverness recovers it.

How often that happens is something we are measuring rather than guessing.

## The two mistakes, and why one is much worse

The component can be wrong in two directions, and they do not cost the same.

**Drawing a shape that should not be there** is usually cheap. A shape that is switched off still
has its control points sitting wherever the artist last left them — normally still inside the
blob the other shapes are already drawing. So drawing it changes almost no pixels.

**Missing a shape that should be there** is expensive. It leaves a hole, and nothing else fills
it.

We measured the ratio: **missing is about 3.2× worse than over-drawing.** That is not a
philosophical point, it decided a design choice — the component is deliberately built to be
eager to switch a shape on and reluctant to switch one off.

## How tight the pass mark is

The pass mark for this stage is: *the picture must stay within 0.01 of where it was.*

We measured what that actually demands before building anything:

| how often the lifespan is right | cost if it over-draws | cost if it misses |
|---|---|---|
| 99% of the time | 0.007 | **0.022** |
| 97% | 0.015 | **0.062** |
| 90% | 0.032 | **0.237** |

At 99% right it is already over budget, if the mistakes are misses.

And more pointedly: if the component gets every shape right but is **one frame early or late** on
when each appears and disappears, that alone costs 0.0102 — the entire budget — or 0.0374 if it
is early.

**So this stage may fail on a margin nobody had measured when the pass mark was written.** We
raised that, and the decision was to keep the mark and report the failure honestly rather than
move the goalposts after seeing how hard the task was. That is the right call: a pass mark
loosened after the fact measures nothing.

---

## The awkward half: point counts

The other thing we are taking away is *how many control points each shape has* — and the score
from that will not mean much. We knew this before running it and said so.

The reason: the model still gets told **which shape is which**, and it keeps a little memory slot
per shape. A point count never changes over time. So the slot can just remember it. That is not
looking at the picture, it is looking up the answer.

For comparison, predictors that *cannot* look up the answer manage 7.7% and 16.4%. A memory slot
manages 100%.

So we build it, report the number, and **gate nothing on it**. The honest version of this test
needs the next stage, where the shape's identity goes away too.

We could have skipped it. We are building it anyway because the component and its decoding have
to exist and be debugged somewhere, and the next stage — where the number finally means
something — is a bad place to be debugging it.

## What "done" looks like

Three training attempts, about 2¼ hours of computer time total:

1. change how it is marked (done — cost nothing, kept)
2. the lifespan component
3. the point-count component

One change at a time, so that if something fails, the log says *which* change failed. Each entry,
with its picture and its cost, is in [s2-attempts.md](s2-attempts.md).
